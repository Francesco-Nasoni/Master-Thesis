\section{ODT loading optimization}
The optimal parameters obtained for the molasses were used for the optimization of the ODT loading. This optimization was mainly focussed on the compensation magnetic field. The idea is to load the MOT and, before switching to the molasses phase, exploit the compensation magnetic field to shift the MOT position.

The MOT is formed where the magnetic field is approximately zero, thus by adjusting the compensation coil currents, it is possible to shift the zero and move the MOT. This makes the MOT loose atom since it is moved away from the optimal position of the intersection of the beams, but there is a trade-off with the better intersection with the ODT beam, that might increase the number of captured atoms. As long as this shift is small the molasses optimal parameters found in the previous sections of this chapter should nullify the residual magnetic field well, and they do not need a second revision.

In particular the compensation coil currents are adjusted to the loading values after load with 200~ms ramp in 20 steps. The time and the ramp are needed to make the translation smooth, in order to minimize the loosing of atom in the process.

As for the molasses, also for the ODT loading preliminary values obtained prior to realignment and molasses optimization were available:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_{\mathrm{x}} &= -200~\mathrm{mA} \\
        I_{\mathrm{y}} &= -350~\mathrm{mA} \\
        I_{\mathrm{z}} &= 50~\mathrm{mA}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:shim_shifted_MOT_preliminary}
\end{equation}

Using an analysis procedure similar to that described in Section~\ref{subsec:odt_loading_analysis_method}, without the rescaling of the background, the data where analyzed to characterize the ODT capturing.
The resulting intensity difference images are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:first_test_fail_2D} (2D image) and in Figure~\ref{fig:first_test_fail_1D} (1D flattened image).
Upon inspection of the 2D image, no brighter area that would represent the atom captured by the ODT is recognizable. By inspecting the 1D image, instead, seem that some pixel ($\sim 4$ pixel) about the fiber position present a higher intensity. Other measurements revealed a similar behavior, confirming that the brightness of these pixels is given by the trapped atom in the ODT.
However, given the small amplitude of the signal, in many images, it was not distinguishable from the noise level. The flatten image are precisely meat to increase the signal-to-noise ratio thus having still a bad signal-to-noise ratio is not good.
Furthermore, the presence of the residual pedestrian that is much higher with respect to the ODT signal is a clue that there are some oscillation noise in the system that are not averaged by the acquisition of five images, either because has a large standard deviation or a correlated behavior form one experiment to another.
The larger concern was though the small ODT signal, which even if the system is supposed to be far from the optimal compensation current was expected to be higher. Another problematic arises, which is the narrowness of the area characterized by the ODT signal. Indeed, if the ODT signal occupies just few pixels the analysis is of particular difficulty because no Gaussian fit are possible, and also calculating the area as the sum of the pixel value carries a lot of uncertainty due to the poor resolution.

This was caused by two factors, the difference of the image with the ODT on and images with ODT off have a residual background large compared with the expected signal of the ODT, therefore the signal is submerged in the noise, second the ODT beam radius is small compared with the cloud radius, therefore the ODT should be visible just in on few number of pixel.


Since the preliminary analysis gave far better results, the parameter of the molasses were systematic changed to verify which caused the problematic. This study brought to the fact that the problematic was caused by the AOM ramp, during the molasses phase. In particular the main problem was caused by the ramp duration: switching from a 20~ms to a 5~ms ramp changed. Furthermore, to reduce as much as possible the fluctuation caused by the AOM switch, also the target amplitude was switched to 480~a.u., which represent the maximum amplitude but for the same reason the point width minimum derivative, thus the point in which the oscillation of the amplitude cause the lower oscillation on the actual power of the beam, stabilizing the system.

The results obtained with this configuration are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:test_good_2D} (2D difference) and \ref{fig:test_good_1D} (1D difference). In the 2D plot a clear brighter strip is recognizable representing the atom captured in the ODT. By comparison of the 1D graphs in the Figures~\ref{fig:first_test_fail_1D} and  \ref{fig:test_good_1D}, it is evident how the pedestrian in the case of 5~ms ramp is smaller, having a maximum and an area of approximately half the case with 20~ms ramp. This is sufficient to make the ODT signal clearly emerge from the residual pedestrian and from the noise.


A problematic is still present which is the fact that with the magnification of the FLIR. For this reason, for the subsequent measurements the Manta camera, that has smaller pixels, was employed, by also mounting it as near as to exploit the minimum working distance of the MVL50M23 objective. By comparing the size of the fiber measured with the Manta and the FLIR, it is deduced that the apparent size of the object in pixels increases by a factor of $\sim 2.25$. This scaling factor arises from the combined contribution of the higher optical magnification and the smaller pixel size of the second imaging system.


\subsubsection{Results}
The systematic optimization of the compensation coil currents was carried out following the complete analysis procedure detailed in Section \ref{subsec:odt_loading_analysis_method}, utilizing the Manta camera. The procedure was performed sequentially along the spatial axes in the order $x \rightarrow z \rightarrow y$. During each scan, the currents of the remaining non-optimized coils were maintained at the preliminary values established in Equation \eqref{eq:shim_shifted_MOT_preliminary}.

The capture efficiencies extracted from these scans are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:shim_capture_efficiencies} for the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-coils. Similarly, Figure~\ref{fig:shim_odt_area} displays the evaluated ODT signal area for each scan. This area is directly proportional to the total number of captured atoms, making it the physical quantity of fundamental interest. However, since the camera was not calibrated for absolute atom number measurements, this quantity can only be expressed in arbitrary units. Nonetheless, these values remain perfectly comparable provided the camera gain is kept constant. As evident from the plots, the behavior of the capture efficiency mirrors the trend of the ODT area.

This initial optimization yielded the following optimal currents:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_{\mathrm{x}} &= -220~\mathrm{mA}, \\
        I_{\mathrm{y}} &= -290~\mathrm{mA}, \\
        I_{\mathrm{z}} &= -90~\mathrm{mA}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

To confirm the optimization achieved in the initial measurements, a second run was performed, yielding unconventional results regarding the $z$-axis compensation coil (Figure \ref{fig:shimZ-2_results}).
As clearly shown in Figure \ref{fig:shimZ-2_signal}, the signal does not follow an expected parabolic or bell-shaped profile with a clear maximum. Instead, the ODT area and the capture efficiency exhibit an oscillatory behavior. Starting from a current of $-1000~\text{mA}$, the signal increases to a first local maximum at $-800~\text{mA}$, drops until $\sim -700~\text{mA}$, and then rises again to reach subsequent local maxima at $\sim -600~\text{mA}$ and $\sim -400~\text{mA}$.

A similar periodic trend is observed in the MOT background area (Figure \ref{fig:shimZ-2_bkg}), which displays peaks at $\sim -800~\text{mA}$, $\sim -600~\text{mA}$, and $\sim -400~\text{mA}$, matching the ODT area oscillation with good precision. While a proportional increase in the captured ODT fraction is naturally expected when the initial MOT atom number is higher, the oscillatory nature of this trend is peculiar. Tracking the $x$-centroid of the atomic cloud on the camera's image plane (Figure \ref{fig:shimZ-2_Cx}) reveals an analogous spatial oscillation, a phenomenon not observed during the scans of the other two compensation axes.
The likely physical explanation for this behavior lies in an imperfect spatial overlap of the cooling beams. 
As the $z$-axis compensation field is scanned, the position of the magnetic zero shifts along the laboratory $z$-axis as expected. However, a non-ideal beam intersection generates multiple localized regions (hotspots) of varying optical intensity. Since the MOT equilibrium position is determined by the balance between the Zeeman-induced magnetic force and the local radiation pressure, the atomic cloud is selectively pushed toward these intensity maxima. This results in a combined motion on the laboratory $z$--$x$ plane, which projects as a spatial oscillation onto the 2D camera sensor.
Furthermore, when the atoms traverse these optimal optical hotspots, the cooling process becomes locally more efficient. This leads to lower temperatures and significantly reduced atom losses, which translates to a higher overall atom number in the MOT (accounting for the peaks in the background signal) and, consequently, an enhanced capture efficiency into the dipole trap.

Finally, the optimal configuration maximizing the ODT loading was found to be:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        I_{\mathrm{x}} &= -220~\mathrm{mA}, \\
        I_{\mathrm{y}} &= -280~\mathrm{mA}, \\
        I_{\mathrm{z}} &= -600~\mathrm{mA}.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This configuration yielded the largest ODT trap area, representing the maximum number of captured atoms, and is associated with a capture efficiency of $(0.160\pm0.005)\%$. The corresponding 2D difference image is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:best_2D_diff}.
